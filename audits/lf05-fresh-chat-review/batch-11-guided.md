## PSY-LF05-02-S1 — Memory, contextual extended writing

**Question:** Discuss how useful the working-memory model is for explaining the results of this investigation.

**Scenario:** A researcher asks 24 sixth-form volunteers to remember six spoken digits while they complete a second task. In Task A, they track the changing positions of coloured shapes on a screen. In Task B, they repeat a sequence of spoken words. Average accuracy on the digit task is 76% in Task A and 49% in Task B. All participants completed both tasks, with Task A always first. The second tasks were not independently matched for difficulty. These figures are invented for the question; they are not real research findings.

**Authored indicative guidance, not an independently accepted mark scheme:**

1. Original Revision 16-mark-style practice and qualitative self-review; not an official AQA question, confirmed item tariff, examiner-calibrated AO split, numeric mark or grade.

2. AO1 / knowledge: Explain the separate roles of the phonological loop (verbal/acoustic rehearsal), visuo-spatial sketchpad (visual/spatial processing), central executive (attentional control/coordination) and, where relevant, episodic buffer (integration). Distinguish their functions rather than presenting separate stores as a fixed proof of the model.

3. AO2 / actual data: The lower digit-task accuracy in Task B (49%) than Task A (76%) is consistent with greater interference when two concurrent tasks draw on verbal processing, compared with combining a verbal and a mainly visual/spatial task. The central executive may coordinate dual-task attention. The data do not isolate every component or measure capacity directly.

4. AO3 / challenge: Because Task A always came first and the secondary tasks were not matched for difficulty, order, fatigue and difficulty could also contribute. The 24 sixth-form volunteers are a known volunteer sample; their narrow population, possible volunteer bias and unspecified recruitment details limit generalisability. Group averages alone cannot isolate a working-memory mechanism. Use dual-task evidence as a general model strength, alongside competing explanations, unclear central-executive boundaries and limitations of simplified laboratory tasks; conclude cautiously.

5. Alternative credit: A well-reasoned argument about attentional demands, task interference or rehearsal is valid if it fits the prompt and does not claim the data alone establish a specific component. The episodic buffer may receive credit if appropriately connected, but it is not mandatory for every answer about this dual-task pattern.

6. What would weaken the answer: stating the numbers prove the working-memory model, confusing the phonological loop with visual processing, or claiming that the Task B accuracy difference was specifically caused by competing verbal processing when task difficulty and fixed task order are confounded. Comparing the task conditions is reasonable; attributing the difference to one component with certainty is not. Also avoid ignoring the investigation's limitations.

7. Useful self-review questions (only after attempt): Did I cite both accuracy values? Did I explain why the task pairing matters? Did I account for the fixed order or unmatched difficulty? Did I use evaluation rather than describe only component definitions?

8. Source-specific correction / alternative-credit safeguard: 76% versus 49% digit accuracy differs by 27 percentage points, but fixed order and unmatched second-task difficulty prevent attributing all of that difference to phonological-loop competition.

9. Qualitative answer-quality check, not a calibrated mark: strong work responds to the exact command and evidence, develops contrasting reasons and a qualified judgement; a list of unrelated definitions is not enough. Accept credible different conclusions supported by the question's facts.
