## PSY-LF05-07 — Design a memory-and-music investigation

**Question:** A researcher wants to test whether background music affects memory. Forty sixth-form volunteers will learn two different word lists, one in silence and one with music, in counterbalanced order. Using the proposed repeated-measures design, describe how the researcher should conduct the study, including controls, and evaluate threats to its validity.

**Scenario:** The same 40 volunteers complete both conditions. List difficulty, musical preferences and time of day could affect recall; the research is not yet underway.

**Authored indicative guidance, not an independently accepted mark scheme:**

1. Training classification: Original Revision 16-mark-style research-methods training task, NOT a verified 16-mark tariff for the 2027 AQA research-methods paper.

2. Research design: Operationalise music versus silence as the independent variable, a scored recall measure as the dependent variable, and an appropriate repeated-measures hypothesis; specify a practical procedure with standardised instructions.

3. Critical list control: Counterbalance both condition order and list assignment. For example, divide the 40 volunteers into four groups of ten: music/List A then silence/List B; silence/List A then music/List B; music/List B then silence/List A; silence/List B then music/List A. Other appropriately balanced assignments earn credit. Pilot-test word lists for similar difficulty; counterbalancing allocation does not itself demonstrate that lists are equally difficult.

4. Applied procedure and ethics: Explain group/order allocation for the 40 volunteers, timing, consistent volume or music conditions, voluntary participation, informed consent and withdrawal, with measures that reduce demand or investigator effects.

5. Evaluation: Assess order/practice/fatigue effects, list equivalence, musical preference, volunteer sampling, ecological/temporal validity and whether observed recall differences would support a causal inference.

6. Alternative credit: A fully specified independent-groups comparison may be evaluated as an alternative with its own individual-differences trade-off; do not treat a replacement design as satisfying the supplied repeated-measures brief without explaining the deviation.

7. Response quality: Strong answers give replicable operational detail, relate each control/weakness to this specific sample and task, and weigh genuine trade-offs. No official examiner mark or numeric AO distribution is inferred.

8. Self-review prompt: Did I counterbalance order AND list-condition assignment, and explain why each identified threat could change the results?

## BUS-LF05-04-25 — Lean production versus other competitiveness tools

**Question:** To what extent is lean production the most effective way for a manufacturer to improve its competitiveness?

**Authored indicative guidance, not an independently accepted mark scheme:**

1. Training classification: Original Revision 25-mark-style Paper 1 broad essay, not an official or independently examiner-calibrated AQA 2027 marking scheme; no supplied case is compulsory.

2. Selected knowledge: Explain lean production, waste reduction, just-in-time versus just-in-case inventory, and efficiency/productivity. Capacity utilisation or quality are useful where directly connected to a causal argument rather than listed as unrelated definitions.

3. Applied examples: Use coherent manufacturer examples (actual or plausible) to distinguish situations with reliable supply, variable demand or tight quality demands. No fictitious case data is provided or required.

4. Developed analysis: Explain plausible cost, responsiveness, lead-time and quality benefits, alongside lean vulnerabilities such as stockouts, supplier disruption, employee capability and excessive pressure on quality.

5. Comparison: Recognise that continuous improvement (kaizen), process improvement and quality management may be part of lean production rather than rival strategies. Contrast genuine competing or complementary priorities such as automation, added flexible capacity or product differentiation; explain when these choices add value or complement lean methods.

6. Balanced judgement: Weigh competitiveness as cost, reliability, quality and differentiation; reach a justified comparative position conditional on supply-chain stability and customer needs.

7. Response quality: Strong answers sustain connected competing causal chains, specific illustration and a justified overall judgement. Lists of lean slogans or unsupported numerical claims are insufficient.

8. Self-review prompt: Did I compare lean with genuine alternatives and explain why the best approach depends on the manufacturer's situation?

**Scenario:** Marshbrook Bakery Ltd has revenue of £1.8m, total costs of £1.74m and £90,000 of cash available. A chain is opening three nearby shops; managers want to borrow £400,000 to expand, while several owners favour slower expansion funded internally to avoid substantial new borrowing.

**Authored indicative guidance, not an independently accepted mark scheme:**

4. AO3 developed causal analysis: Explain how insufficient cash buffers could constrain wages, suppliers or investment despite reported profit; the £90,000 cash balance cannot by itself establish whether the buffer is adequate without payment timings and obligations. 5. **Authored indicative guidance, not an independently accepted mark scheme:**

1. Original Revision 16-mark-style practice and qualitative self-review; not an official AQA question, confirmed item tariff, examiner-calibrated AO split, numeric mark or grade.

2. AO1 accurate concepts: Explain training, turnover, motivation, retention and the difference between on-the-job and off-the-job development.

If every leaver is replaced and trained at £1,200, costs could be £69,120 using the unrounded estimate or £69,600 using 58 rounded recruits; accept both well-explained estimates. Recruitment and training actually depend on replacements and needs, which are unknown. Customer satisfaction declined from 86% to 77% (9 percentage points).

4. AO3 developed causal analysis: Link higher competence to service quality and recruitment costs, but distinguish inadequate training from poor scheduling and progression. Explain time and opportunity costs plus the risk trained employees still leave.

5. AO4 evaluation and judgement: Credit either targeted/phase-in training, or a justified rota/progression-first response, or a combination, if it weighs the case facts and trade-offs. ## PSY-LF05-02-S1 — Memory, contextual extended writing

**Question:** Discuss how useful the working-memory model is for explaining the results of this investigation.

**Scenario:** A researcher asks 24 sixth-form volunteers to remember six spoken digits while they complete a second task. In Task A, they track the changing positions of coloured shapes on a screen. In Task B, they repeat a sequence of spoken words. Average accuracy on the digit task is 76% in Task A and 49% in Task B. All participants completed both tasks, with Task A always first. The second tasks were not independently matched for difficulty. These figures are invented for the question; they are not real research findings.

**Authored indicative guidance, not an independently accepted mark scheme:**

1. Original Revision 16-mark-style practice and qualitative self-review; not an official AQA question, confirmed item tariff, examiner-calibrated AO split, numeric mark or grade.

2. AO1 / knowledge: Explain the separate roles of the phonological loop (verbal/acoustic rehearsal), visuo-spatial sketchpad (visual/spatial processing), central executive (attentional control/coordination) and, where relevant, episodic buffer (integration). Distinguish their functions rather than presenting separate stores as a fixed proof of the model.

3. AO2 / actual data: The lower digit-task accuracy in Task B (49%) than Task A (76%) is consistent with greater interference when two concurrent tasks draw on verbal processing, compared with combining a verbal and a mainly visual/spatial task. The central executive may coordinate dual-task attention. The data do not isolate every component or measure capacity directly.

4. AO3 / challenge: Because Task A always came first and the secondary tasks were not matched for difficulty, order, fatigue and difficulty could also contribute. The 24 sixth-form volunteers are a known volunteer sample; their narrow population, possible volunteer bias and unspecified recruitment details limit generalisability. Group averages alone cannot isolate a working-memory mechanism. Use dual-task evidence as a general model strength, alongside competing explanations, unclear central-executive boundaries and limitations of simplified laboratory tasks; conclude cautiously.

5. Alternative credit: A well-reasoned argument about attentional demands, task interference or rehearsal is valid if it fits the prompt and does not claim the data alone establish a specific component. The episodic buffer may receive credit if appropriately connected, but it is not mandatory for every answer about this dual-task pattern.

6. What would weaken the answer: stating the numbers prove the working-memory model, confusing the phonological loop with visual processing, or claiming that the Task B accuracy difference was specifically caused by competing verbal processing when task difficulty and fixed task order are confounded. Comparing the task conditions is reasonable; attributing the difference to one component with certainty is not. Also avoid ignoring the investigation's limitations.

7. Useful self-review questions (only after attempt): Did I cite both accuracy values? Did I explain why the task pairing matters? Did I account for the fixed order or unmatched difficulty? Did I use evaluation rather than describe only component definitions?

8. Source-specific correction / alternative-credit safeguard: 76% versus 49% digit accuracy differs by 27 percentage points, but fixed order and unmatched second-task difficulty prevent attributing all of that difference to phonological-loop competition.

9. Qualitative answer-quality check, not a calibrated mark: strong work responds to the exact command and evidence, develops contrasting reasons and a qualified judgement; a list of unrelated definitions is not enough. Accept credible different conclusions supported by the question's facts.
