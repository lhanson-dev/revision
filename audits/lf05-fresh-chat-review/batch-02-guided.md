## BUS-LF05-03-16 — Mira Sports Drinks: product or price?

**Question:** Evaluate whether Mira Sports Drinks should introduce a sugar-free premium product rather than cut prices on its current range.

**Scenario:** Mira Sports Drinks has a 12% market share. Sales volume fell 8% after a £1 price rise. A survey of 80 urban athletes finds strong interest in a sugar-free version, but rivals now promote cheaper alternatives.

**Authored indicative guidance, not an independently accepted mark scheme:**

1. Training classification: Original Revision 16-mark-style contextual Business evaluation; AQA's exact 2027 item-level AO split is not verified and no official mark is awarded.

2. Accurate knowledge: Explain relevant segmentation, targeting and positioning alongside product and price elements of the marketing mix and price sensitivity.

3. Case application: Use Mira's 12% share, 8% sales-volume fall after a £1 price rise, interest in sugar-free products among 80 urban athletes, and cheaper rival offers. The survey is limited and may not represent the wider market.

4. Developed analysis: Explain plausible benefits of premium sugar-free differentiation versus reducing prices to defend sales volumes and share; connect customer demand, margins and brand position, using conditional causal reasoning.

5. Evidence boundaries: The stimulus does not supply contribution per unit, production capacity, launch cost or a reliable price elasticity estimate. Discuss these as unknown decision risks, not established facts or calculable certainties.

6. Reasoned judgement: Compare both choices and justify a conditional recommendation linked to contribution, representativeness of market testing and competitor response. Credit defensible alternatives on the same evidence.

7. Response quality: Strong answers use multiple real case facts, reason through competing consequences and support a qualified judgement; a generic list of four Ps or unsupported claims about profitability remains limited.

8. Self-review prompt: Did I make a genuine choice, treat sample and price-change evidence cautiously, and avoid inventing margin or production-capacity figures?

## BUS-LF05-04-25 — Lean production versus other competitiveness tools

**Question:** To what extent is lean production the most effective way for a manufacturer to improve its competitiveness?

**Authored indicative guidance, not an independently accepted mark scheme:**

1. Training classification: Original Revision 25-mark-style Paper 1 broad essay, not an official or independently examiner-calibrated AQA 2027 marking scheme; no supplied case is compulsory.

2. Selected knowledge: Explain lean production, waste reduction, just-in-time versus just-in-case inventory, and efficiency/productivity. Capacity utilisation or quality are useful where directly connected to a causal argument rather than listed as unrelated definitions.

3. Applied examples: Use coherent manufacturer examples (actual or plausible) to distinguish situations with reliable supply, variable demand or tight quality demands. No fictitious case data is provided or required.

4. Developed analysis: Explain plausible cost, responsiveness, lead-time and quality benefits, alongside lean vulnerabilities such as stockouts, supplier disruption, employee capability and excessive pressure on quality.

5. Comparison: Recognise that continuous improvement (kaizen), process improvement and quality management may be part of lean production rather than rival strategies. Contrast genuine competing or complementary priorities such as automation, added flexible capacity or product differentiation; explain when these choices add value or complement lean methods.

6. Balanced judgement: Weigh competitiveness as cost, reliability, quality and differentiation; reach a justified comparative position conditional on supply-chain stability and customer needs.

7. Response quality: Strong answers sustain connected competing causal chains, specific illustration and a justified overall judgement. Lists of lean slogans or unsupported numerical claims are insufficient.

8. Self-review prompt: Did I compare lean with genuine alternatives and explain why the best approach depends on the manufacturer's situation?

## BUS-LF05-07-25 — Quantitative versus qualitative strategic analysis

**Question:** To what extent are quantitative methods more valuable than qualitative assessment when managers analyse a business's strategic position?

**Authored indicative guidance, not an independently accepted mark scheme:**

1. Training classification: Original Revision 25-mark-style Paper 1 strategic analysis essay; the precise AQA 2027 item-level tariff and AO marks are not verified.

2. Selected knowledge: Explain how quantitative ratios, trend forecasts and selected investment-appraisal estimates differ from qualitative information about customers, management, culture and competitive context; distinguish a tool from the evidence it contains.

3. Relevant context: Illustrate comparisons using reasonable contrasting business situations, without inventing a compulsory supplied case or assuming a named company's private figures.

4. Developed analysis: Quantitative evidence can support comparison, measurement and prioritisation but ratios depend on accounts and definitions while forecasts/appraisals rest on uncertain assumptions. Explain which questions the numbers can and cannot answer.

5. Qualitative balance: Analyse how stakeholder insight, culture, SWOT/PEST-style judgements and strategic capabilities add context. SWOT and external-analysis tools may mix numeric and qualitative evidence; do not misclassify them as inherently quantitative.

6. Balanced judgement: Compare methods for distinct purposes, industry conditions, scale and uncertainty; a mixed approach may be strongest, but credit a reasoned quantitatively or qualitatively weighted judgement.

7. Response quality: Strong answers develop competing chains with relevant examples, interrogate evidence limitations and sustain a conditional judgement, not a list of tools or an asserted universal ranking.

8. Self-review prompt: Did I distinguish measurement from judgement, explain forecast uncertainty and consider genuinely qualitative factors in the specific strategic decision?
