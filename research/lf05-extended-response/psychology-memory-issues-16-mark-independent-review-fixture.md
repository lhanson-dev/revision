## Fixture M — `PSY-LF05-02`: Memory, Working-Memory Model

**Original prompt:** Discuss the working-memory model as an explanation of how people can carry out different short-term memory tasks at the same time. **[16 marks — training only]**

**Expected source facts to challenge, not a mark checklist:** central executive, phonological loop, visuospatial sketchpad, episodic buffer, coding/capacity; why concurrent activities may interfere differently; strengths and limitations of the explanatory model. The PDF does **not** prescribe named dual-task experiments or patient cases.

### M-H: relatively developed original student-style response (unmarked anchor)

The working-memory model proposes that short-term processing is not a single store doing one task at a time. A central executive directs attention and coordinates specialised components. The phonological loop holds and rehearses verbal material, while the visuospatial sketchpad maintains and manipulates visual or spatial information. The episodic buffer is proposed to bring information together across sources and link it with long-term knowledge. Each subsystem has limitations, so whether two activities can be performed at once depends partly on the resources they draw upon.

For example, repeating a spoken sequence while mentally tracking a visual path could be more manageable than following two different verbal streams together. That fits the model's prediction that two tasks relying on the same restricted processing resource are more likely to interfere. Results from dual-task procedures can therefore support a division between verbal and visual-spatial processing. But successful simultaneous performance does not by itself prove the precise set of components: the tasks may be unequal in familiarity, complexity or attentional demand.

Another strength is that some people show a relatively selective difficulty with verbal short-term memory while other abilities are less affected. Such differences are difficult for a completely undifferentiated short-term store to explain. Nevertheless, a selective impairment is not proof that a neatly separated component fully accounts for ordinary learning in every setting.

A weakness is that the central executive's control function is broad and harder to specify precisely than storage in the two more specialised components. If an unexpected task result is always attributed to a shift in executive attention, the theory risks explaining outcomes after the event rather than making discriminating predictions. The episodic buffer also illustrates that the model has required refinement to explain integrated experiences.

Overall the model gives a useful explanation of why distinct short-term activities may be coordinated without equal interference, but its value depends on task-specific evidence and it is less precise about how executive control and integration operate. It explains important features of short-term processing rather than every aspect of memory.

**Reviewer probes:** Does the response distinguish *holding material* from *processing* it? Is the central executive incorrectly portrayed as an unlimited store? Do verbal-vs-visual dual-task examples imply unsupported experimental numbers? Is episodic-buffer integration described with calibrated uncertainty? Is evaluation connected to the *concurrent-task* command rather than generic WMM criticism?

### M-M: partially developed original response (unmarked anchor)

Working memory has different parts, so two short-term tasks do not always compete for exactly the same space. The phonological loop deals with sounds and words, and the visuospatial sketchpad is for pictures and positions. The central executive directs the work, while the episodic buffer joins information. This can explain why someone might remember a spoken number while looking for a route.

Researchers can give people two tasks together. If they do two different kinds of tasks better than two similar tasks, this supports the model. One problem is that the central executive is not described very clearly. The model also does not explain everything about long-term memory. So it is a useful explanation of short-term tasks, but not perfect.

**Reviewer probes:** Valid central concept, but little discussion of finite capacity, task difficulty, robustness of evidence, counterargument or integration. Is the essay a weak developed answer rather than merely a list? What additional causal explanation would move it upwards? Do not automatically place in a precise 16-mark band before independent calibration.

### M-L: superficial original response (unmarked anchor)

The working-memory model says memory has different parts, like the phonological loop and central executive. One is about words and one controls things. This means you can do two things at once because there are different bits of memory. It is a good model because it explains short-term memory.

**Reviewer probes:** It names components but does not adequately distinguish all four, evaluate evidence, identify capacity limits or qualify the sweeping claim about two tasks. Credit relevant fragments without inventing unseen research or a precise grade.

## Fixture I — `PSY-LF05-08`: Issues and Debates, Nature–Nurture

**Original prompt:** Discuss the nature–nurture debate in psychology, using relevant examples from other topics of psychology you have studied. **[16 marks — training only]**

**Expected source facts to challenge, not a mark checklist:** heredity/biological influences, environmental influences and interactionism, with appropriate links to *actually studied* Psychology topics. §4.3 explicitly encourages links to other studied topics **as appropriate**; it does **not** impose a universal minimum topic count or make a particular optional Paper 3 choice compulsory.

### I-H: relatively developed original student-style response (unmarked anchor)

The nature–nurture debate asks how far behaviour is influenced by inherited or biological processes and how far it develops through experience and environment. A purely nature-based explanation can give biological differences a major role; a purely nurture-based one gives priority to learning and circumstances. These are useful contrasting starting points, but interactionism suggests that behaviour often reflects both kinds of influence acting together.

The behavioural account of phobias, studied in Clinical Psychology and Mental Health, illustrates a strong nurture explanation. Classical conditioning can link a previously neutral stimulus with fear, while avoiding the stimulus can be reinforced because avoiding it reduces anxiety. This has practical value because it gives a reason for treatment strategies that change learned reactions. Yet this cannot establish that every person has the same susceptibility or that every fear begins through a directly experienced conditioning event. Learning offers one important route, not a complete explanation of individual differences.

The biological approach to obsessive-compulsive disorder draws attention to genetic and neural influences. Such influences can contribute to vulnerability and encourage investigation of underlying mechanisms. However, finding an association does not show that a person was destined to develop the disorder. Families also share environments, while the relationship between biology and lived experience can be complex. A biological predisposition might affect sensitivity to circumstances without replacing the need to study those circumstances.

Attachment also shows why a simple opposition can mislead. Bowlby's approach proposes an evolved basis for attachment, but the developing internal working model and the quality of early caregiving experiences help explain why relationships differ. Calling that explanation entirely inherited or entirely learned obscures the combination of tendencies and experience. Different explanations can emphasise different points without implying that one example settles the whole debate.

Interactionism can therefore offer a stronger general account, provided researchers do not invoke a vague 'mixture of both' without specifying mechanisms or suitable evidence. The debate remains useful because identifying biological constraints and changeable environmental influences affects both research design and intervention. Its main limitation is the temptation to frame a complex developmental process as a competition with only one winner.

**Reviewer probes:** Are phobia conditioning, OCD biological explanations and Bowlby accurately characterised for the updated compulsory specification? Does the response wrongly use treatment success as proof of original cause? Does it overclaim from family designs or genetic associations? Are the three topic examples substantive and evaluated, rather than counted as three automatic marks? Does interactionism offer a meaningful argument rather than a vague compromise?

### I-M: partially developed original response (unmarked anchor)

Nature means things we inherit or are born with, and nurture means what happens to us in the environment. Both can affect behaviour. For example, some biological explanations of OCD discuss genes and the nervous system, while phobias can be explained by classical conditioning. The two-process model also explains why avoiding a feared object may continue because it reduces fear.

This shows that psychology has different ways of explaining behaviour. Biological explanations can be useful because they investigate how the body works, but they may underestimate experiences. Environmental explanations are useful because they show how behaviour can change, though they do not explain why everyone reacts differently. Interactionism combines these ideas and is probably better than always choosing one.

**Reviewer probes:** Correct baseline contrast and relevant studied examples, but under-developed mechanisms, evidence quality, interactionist details and sustained judgement. Do not treat merely naming two topics as satisfying evaluation.

### I-L: superficial original response (unmarked anchor)

Nature is genes and nurture is the environment. Nature makes people act in ways they are born with. Nurture is like learning from people. Attachment is about parents and that is nurture. Both are important in psychology and neither is always right.

**Reviewer probes:** Some relevant terminology, but the response is largely assertion, with no developed interactionism, no accurate explanation of attachment theory, no evidential challenge and no evaluated synoptic argument. Avoid a precise mark without independent anchor calibration.
