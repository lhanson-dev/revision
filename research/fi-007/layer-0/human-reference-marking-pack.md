## Harbour Home Ltd case

Harbour Home Ltd (HH) is a UK private limited company selling modular home-office furniture online. It was founded six years ago by Maya and Ben, who each own 50% of the shares. Revenue has grown rapidly as hybrid working has become common, but management is now concerned that growth is creating operational and financial pressure.

HH manufactures desks and storage units in a rented factory. In 2025 it sold 18,000 units. The average selling price was £180, total variable costs were £1.89m and fixed costs were £720,000. In 2026 maximum annual capacity is 24,000 units and forecast output is 22,800 units. Customer reviews remain strong overall, but complaints about late delivery have doubled.

HH currently holds significant timber and component inventory because one key supplier has a long and unreliable lead time. The operations director proposes investing £500,000 in automated cutting equipment. She predicts this would increase capacity by 20%, reduce waste and lower variable cost per unit. Some experienced production employees fear the equipment will reduce job security. Labour turnover has already risen from 8% to 15%.

The marketing director wants to launch a premium range aimed at professional designers. Research suggests the segment values customisation and sustainable materials more than low prices. She proposes a high initial price and a relationship-marketing programme for repeat trade customers. Competitors are also entering the premium segment.

HH forecasts a temporary cash deficit if it buys the equipment immediately. A bank has offered a five-year loan. An investor has offered the full £500,000 in exchange for 20% of HH and a board seat. Maya prefers to retain control; Ben believes the investor’s e-commerce experience could accelerate growth.

Industry data: the UK home-office furniture market index is 100 in 2024, 111 in 2025 and 123 in 2026. HH sales index is 100 in 2024, 119 in 2025 and 128 in 2026.

**Question:** Analyse the impact on HH of holding significant inventory.

**AOs:** AO1 3 · AO2 3 · AO3 3

It may also tie up cash and create storage/waste risk, particularly because HH forecasts a cash deficit. Developed chains should reach customer service, competitiveness, cash flow or profit.

**Answer:**

Holding lots of inventory can help a business because it means it is less likely to run out of materials. This means production can continue and customers can get their orders. It can also be a disadvantage because inventory costs money and takes up space. If too much stock is held then some of it could be wasted or damaged. Overall, keeping inventory is useful if the business needs to make sure production does not stop, but it should not hold too much because this could increase costs.

## TC-16B — 16 marks

**Question:** Do you think HH should use the bank loan or the investor to finance the automation? Justify your answer.

**AOs:** AO1 4 · AO2 4 · AO3 4 · AO4 4

**Marking guidance:** Analyse both options rather than isolated pros/cons. A loan preserves Maya/Ben’s control but creates interest/repayment pressure while cash is forecast to be tight. The investor reduces scheduled repayment pressure and brings e-commerce experience but requires 20% ownership and a board seat. Reach a contextual judgement based on cash resilience, control objectives, cost of finance and the strategic value of expertise.

**Answer:**

Using the bank loan would allow Maya and Ben to keep control of HH. This matters because Maya does not want to give an outside investor 20% of the company or a board seat. If the automation succeeds and reduces waste and variable costs, HH could use the extra cash generated to meet the loan repayments without permanently giving away ownership. Interest and repayments could make that position worse, especially if the premium range does not grow as quickly as expected.

The investor would avoid fixed loan repayments and could therefore protect short-term cash flow. The investor also has e-commerce experience, which could be valuable because HH sells online and wants to grow. On the other hand, Maya and Ben would lose some control and future profits would be shared with another owner.

I would choose the investor if HH's cash-flow forecast shows that loan repayments leave very little safety margin. If the cash deficit is small and temporary, the loan is probably better because the founders retain ownership. The decision therefore depends mainly on how severe the cash-flow pressure is and how valuable the investor's expertise would actually be.

## TC-20C — 20 marks

**Question:** “For a growing business such as HH, operational performance is more important than marketing performance in determining long-term success.” To what extent do you agree?

**AOs:** AO1 4 · AO2 3 · AO3 5 · AO4 8

**Marking guidance:** Draw together operations, marketing, finance and HR where relevant. Operations evidence includes 95% utilisation, late deliveries, supplier risk, automation, capacity and cost. Marketing evidence includes premium positioning, new segment, competition, relationship marketing and willingness to pay. Evaluate interdependence and finish with a clear conditional judgement.

**Answer:**

For HH, operational performance is currently the tighter constraint on long-term success, but saying it is generally more important than marketing would be too simple. HH's sales index has risen faster than the wider market and forecast capacity utilisation is 95%, while complaints about late delivery have doubled. This suggests demand already exists and operations may be preventing HH from converting that demand into satisfied customers and repeat sales. Automation could raise capacity, reduce waste and lower variable cost per unit, so improving operations could protect both service and margin.

However, operational improvements create value only if HH continues to generate profitable demand. The proposed premium range targets designers who care about customisation and sustainable materials. Good marketing could therefore allow HH to charge a higher price and avoid competing mainly on cost. Relationship marketing could also be important because repeat professional customers may be valuable. If competitors enter with better-designed premium offers, extra production capacity alone would not protect HH.

The two functions also interact. Successful premium marketing could worsen HH's existing capacity problem if orders increase faster than production capability. Equally, spending £500,000 on automation before proving premium demand could create financial pressure because HH already forecasts a temporary cash deficit. HR matters too: rising labour turnover and job-security fears could undermine the benefits of new technology if skilled employees leave or resist implementation.

I therefore agree only for HH's present situation. Its immediate operational problems threaten the customer experience despite strong demand, so fixing capacity, delivery reliability and waste should come first. Over the longer term, the priority should shift according to the constraint: once operational reliability is restored, marketing performance may become equally or more important for sustaining growth and defending the premium position. Long-term success depends less on choosing one function permanently and more on preventing one weak function from limiting the others.
