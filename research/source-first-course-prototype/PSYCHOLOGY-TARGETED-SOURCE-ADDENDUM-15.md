### Course Truth bridge

Human food preference reflects interacting biological preparedness, learning and culture rather than one evolved fixed menu. **Food neophobia** can be understood as a cautious response to unfamiliar foods that may have offered ancestral protection from toxins/pathogens, while **taste aversion** shows biologically prepared associative learning in which illness can rapidly produce avoidance of a recently consumed flavour.

Food preferences are also learned through repeated exposure, direct reinforcement, modelling of parents/peers and culturally available food practices. Evolutionary predisposition does not make preference immutable; social and cultural learning can amplify, redirect or override initial tendencies.

## PSY-13-04 — psychological explanations of anorexia nervosa

**Previous live status:** `partial`  
**Continuation status:** `covered_candidate`

### Family systems source

Vanderlinden (2026), *Family-based therapy for eating disorders: from the Milan model to contemporary evidence*  
DOI: `10.3389/frcha.2026.1765146`  
URL: https://doi.org/10.3389/frcha.2026.1765146  
Classification: `OPEN`  
Licence: `CC BY`  
Rights evidence: Frontiers/PMC explicitly distributes the article under Creative Commons Attribution.  
Supports:

- historical systemic accounts associated with Selvini Palazzoli and Minuchin;
- structural-family concepts including enmeshment/blurred boundaries and relational organisation;
- autonomy/control tensions in systemic conceptualisations; and

### Social-learning/media sources

DOI: `10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01351`

### Cognitive source

Treasure et al. (2020), *Cognitive Interpersonal Model for Anorexia Nervosa Revisited*  
URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7141127/  
Classification: `OPEN`  
Licence: `CC BY 4.0`  
Supports cognitive rigidity, attentional/interpretive biases, perfectionism and self-reinforcing cognitive/interpersonal processes that can maintain restrictive eating and anorexia nervosa.

### Course Truth bridge

Historical **family systems theory** focused on patterns such as enmeshment, blurred boundaries, overprotectiveness, conflict avoidance, autonomy and control. These concepts belong in the course because they are named theoretical explanations, but they must not be converted into a claim that a particular family structure causes anorexia nervosa. Contemporary understanding is biopsychosocial, and family-based treatment explicitly avoids parent blame.

The **social-learning explanation** applies modelling, imitation and reinforcement to eating/body-related behaviour. Family, peer and media models can make thinness, dieting or restrictive behaviour salient; observed social reward can reinforce those standards. Evidence linking social-media use to thin-ideal internalisation supports media influence, but association does not establish that media exposure alone causes anorexia nervosa.

The **cognitive explanation** focuses on distorted or rigid beliefs and information-processing biases involving weight, shape, control, self-worth and food. Restriction and starvation can themselves intensify cognitive rigidity and preoccupation, creating maintenance cycles. Cognitive distortion is therefore treated as a mechanism within a complex disorder, not as a claim that the person simply chooses irrational thinking.

## PSY-13-06 — restraint theory, disinhibition and boundary model

**Previous live status:** `partial`  
**Continuation status:** `covered_candidate`

### Accepted open sources

Adams et al. (2019), *Do restrained eaters show increased BMI, food craving and disinhibited eating?*  
URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6599805/  
Classification: `OPEN`  
Licence: `CC BY 4.0`  
Supports the restrained-eating construct, differences between restraint measures and relationships among restraint, craving and disinhibited eating.

Stroebe et al. / Frontiers (2023), *Hunger modulates perceptions of food health but not taste in restricted eaters*  
URL: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1212778/full  
Classification: `OPEN`  
Licence: `CC BY`  
Supports restrained eating and explicitly describes Herman and Polivy’s boundary model, including cognitive dietary restraint, altered hunger/satiety thresholds and the possibility of overeating when the diet boundary is transgressed.

Stroebe (2022), *The goal conflict model: A theory of hedonic regulation of eating behavior*  
DOI: `10.1016/j.cobeha.2022.101203`  
Classification: `OPEN`  
Licence: `CC BY`  
Supports a modern critical account of the boundary model: restrained eaters can maintain cognitive control while restraint is focal, but strong emotion or perceived dietary violation can undermine control and produce overeating; it also documents limitations and alternative explanation through competing eating-enjoyment and weight-control goals.

### Course Truth bridge

**Restraint theory** proposes that deliberate cognitive restriction can make eating depend more heavily on self-imposed rules than ordinary hunger/satiety regulation. **Disinhibition** is the breakdown or release of that restraint, potentially producing overeating after perceived dietary violation, emotional disruption or reduced cognitive control.

The **boundary model** represents eating between biological hunger and satiety boundaries, with restrained eaters adding a self-imposed cognitive diet boundary intended to stop eating before physiological satiety. Crossing or abandoning that diet boundary can produce counter-regulation or disinhibited eating in the model.

These models are historically influential but not uncontested. Modern evidence shows that restrained eating is heterogeneous and that different restraint measures identify different patterns. Revision therefore teaches the named theory and predictions while preserving empirical limitations rather than presenting restraint as a universal route to obesity.
