# Lesson — Why strategic change succeeds or fails

## Outcomes

Explain why change is needed and why it may fail; analyse organisational culture; explain and evaluate approaches to managing resistance and implementation; interpret critical paths and contingency risks where required by the **7132** course; and apply change management to an unfamiliar UK business.

### 1. Why businesses change

A business might change strategy because customer needs move, technology changes production, regulation alters costs, competitors enter or its own financial performance weakens. A strategic change affects long-term direction, objectives, business activities or capabilities. Not every operational adjustment changes strategy.

**Example:** A UK chain of independent bicycle workshops finds customers increasingly want online bookings, home collection and rapid repair status updates. Its owners consider introducing centralised booking software, staff training and changed opening patterns across 12 sites. Those are connected changes to systems, roles and how the business delivers value.

Change can be **proactive** (anticipating a trend and acting early) or **reactive** (responding to a decline or threat). Acting quickly can protect competitive position, but a rushed change may fail when staff capacity, cash flow or customer behaviour is misunderstood.

**Trade-off:** Transformation creates immediate costs with uncertain later benefits. Assessment should link speed, finance, people and feasibility to the case—not assume all change is beneficial.

### 2. Business cultures and strategic implementation

**Organisational culture** includes shared assumptions, norms and behaviours shaping how people decide and work. A mismatch between the culture and a new strategy can make implementation difficult. A hierarchical business may coordinate safety and consistency well but resist local experimentation; a collaborative team may respond quickly but struggle with unclear accountability. No single culture is automatically best.

For **7132 study**, Handy's commonly taught four cultural patterns are:
- **Power culture:** key decisions concentrate around influential central people; can be quick but dependent on a few leaders.
- **Role culture:** authority follows defined jobs, rules and procedures; supports predictability but may become rigid.
- **Task culture:** temporary expertise-based groups form around tasks or projects; can adapt but needs coordination.
- **Person culture:** the organisation exists primarily around the autonomy and interests of professional individuals; can make collective direction difficult.

These are analytical descriptions, not claims that every organisation fits neatly into one box. The four named patterns are **not explicitly prescribed in 7138**; a 7138 learner instead needs grounded analysis of why culture helps or hinders change.

**Application:** A role-based repair chain can safeguard quality standards but might struggle to empower front-line technicians to test a new collection service. An answer should explain *why*, with evidence about roles, incentives, systems and customer expectations.

### 3. Diagnosing resistance (Kotter and Schlesinger)

Four reasons commonly distinguished in the model are:
1. **Self-interest:** employees may lose influence, income, schedule flexibility or opportunities.
2. **Misunderstanding and lack of trust:** people may not believe the rationale or promises.
3. **Different assessments:** employees may see risks and alternatives leaders have missed.
4. **Low tolerance for change:** people may fear they lack the skills, time or support.

These reasons imply different responses. A worker worried about reduced overtime may need a negotiated adjustment; a colleague unsure how new software works needs training; a manager who sees a genuine safety risk should be heard rather than dismissed.

For **7132**, the six well-known strategies for responding are **education/communication, participation/involvement, facilitation/support, negotiation/agreement, manipulation/co-optation, and explicit/implicit coercion**. None is always best:
- Communication improves understanding but may not resolve a genuine pay loss.
- Participation can improve design and buy-in but consumes time.
- Support and training address capability gaps but require resources.
- Negotiation can resolve material losses but adds cost and may set precedents.
- Manipulation can obtain short-term compliance but undermines trust and presents ethical risks.
- Coercion can impose a rapid deadline but damages relationships and may increase turnover.

**7138 boundary:** its named learning requirement is the *four reasons for resistance*, not the six named methods. Any adaptation must keep those two sets separate.

### 4. Lewin's force-field analysis

List the forces **driving** a change and those **restraining** it. The logic is to strengthen credible drivers and, often more sustainably, reduce restraints rather than simply apply pressure.

**Worked illustrative example (not objective numerical measurement):**

| Drivers for online-booking rollout | Strength | Restraints | Strength |
| --- | ---: | --- | ---: |
| Customer demand for visibility | 4 | Limited software skills | 4 |
| Competitors offer online tracking | 3 | Cost of implementation | 3 |
| Better appointment utilisation | 3 | Staff concern about schedules | 2 |
| **Total** | **10** | **Total** | **9** |

The numbers are qualitative management judgements, not scientific units. The nominal difference of one is **not** proof the initiative will succeed. If training reduces skill-related resistance from 4 to 2, the change could become easier, but the business must still assess financing and implementation risk.

**Evaluation:** Force-field analysis structures discussion of competing influences, but subjective ratings can conceal uncertainty or politics. It does not calculate an objective probability of success.

### 5. Strategic implementation, network analysis and contingencies (7132 emphasis)

Even a sensible strategy fails without responsibilities, resource allocation, deadlines, communication and monitoring. A **critical path** is the sequence of activities controlling the earliest possible completion time in a dependent project network; a delay on that path delays completion unless other changes offset it. **Total float** is how long a task can be delayed without delaying overall completion. Never call a task critical just because it feels important.

**Original small worked example:**

| Task | Dependency | Duration (days) | Earliest finish |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: |
| A Choose software | — | 3 | 3 |
| B Confirm site equipment | A | 4 | 7 |
| C Prepare staff guide | A | 5 | 8 |
| D Train team | B, C | 3 | 11 |
| E Launch | D | 1 | 12 |

Two routes are A–B–D–E = 11 days and A–C–D–E = 12 days. Thus **A–C–D–E** is the critical path for this simplified network (12 days). B finishes on day 7 but D must also wait for C (day 8), so B has **one day total float**. Note that a complete network calculation should explicitly include earliest/latest start and finish; this table is introductory, not a complete AQA diagram.

**Contingency planning:** identify disruptions (vendor delay, staff turnover, data migration problems), assess consequences, choose proportionate alternatives and establish trigger points. A contingency is not a substitute for prevention; carrying excess backup capacity also costs money.

### 6. Pulling it together

A convincing analysis traces **cause → impact → relevant management response → likely consequence → limitation**. For the workshop chain, uncertainty about new shifts may create self-interest resistance; a timetable pilot and negotiated shifts could reduce it. But if cash is tight, delaying rollout may protect liquidity while ceding customers to digital rivals. Which dominates depends on the numbers, capacity and competitive situation.

**Recap:** Strategic change requires clear reasoning, cultural fit, diagnosis of resistance, balanced implementation, realistic scheduling and risk management. Named framework coverage is qualification-specific.

# Ten-question quiz with feedback

*Original Revision quiz, one mark each. No official AQA questions used.*

1. **Which is strategic rather than a routine operational adjustment?** A Changing today's break rota; B Entering a new market with a different business model; C Replacing a broken screwdriver; D Correcting a typo. **B** — it changes long-term direction.
2. **Which illustrates proactive change?** A Upgrading booking before customer departures accelerate; B Repairing flood damage after closure; C Replacing lost receipts; D Responding to yesterday's outage. **A** — anticipates future pressure.
3. **Culture chiefly concerns...** A only written policy; B only payroll; C shared norms and behaviours; D legal ownership form. **C** — behaviour and assumptions often exceed written rules.
4. **For 7132, which pattern best describes a heavily rule-governed hierarchy?** A Person; B Role; C Task; D Market. **B** — formal roles and procedures dominate.
5. **Workers fear loss of overtime earnings. Which resistance reason best fits?** A Self-interest; B Confusion alone; C Different technical forecasts only; D Lack of literacy. **A** — potential personal loss matters.
6. **Workers understand the strategy but believe managers have overlooked a safety problem. Which reason fits?** A Automatically self-interest; B Different assessment; C No effect; D Only low tolerance. **B** — their evidence/assessment differs.
7. **Which action primarily reduces limited skills as a restraining force?** A Make the deadline earlier; B Hide costs; C Provide relevant training and support; D Ignore complaints. **C** — addresses capability.
8. **What is a key weakness of assigning numbers in force-field analysis?** A All forces must be equal; B Figures are judgemental, not objective measurements; C No driver can be reduced; D It always proves a probability. **B** — scoring can mislead.
9. **Tasks A (3d), C (5d after A), D (3d after B and C), E (1d after D); B takes 4d after A. What is the minimum completion time?** A 8; B 10; C 11; D 12. **D** — A–C–D–E = 12 days, longer than the B route.
10. **Why plan an alternate booking channel before rollout?** A To guarantee demand; B To eliminate all cost; C To prepare for a software failure; D To avoid training entirely. **C** — it is contingency for a plausible disruption.

# Original 15-mark Business 7138-style question and experimental marking guide

**Original mini-case:**

Coastline Cycles Ltd operates 12 bicycle-repair outlets across Northern England. Annual bookings have fallen by 8%, and competitors now offer real-time repair tracking. Directors want to replace telephone bookings with one central online platform within four months. The software provider estimates implementation will cost £95,000. A third of staff have raised concerns about reduced overtime and inadequate training. Branch managers disagree about whether the proposed schedule allows enough time for a safe rollout. The company has modest cash reserves but expects better capacity utilisation once the platform is working.

**Question (original; 15 marks):** *Evaluate whether Coastline Cycles Ltd should proceed with its planned four-month introduction of the central online platform.*

This is an experimental **7138-aligned evaluate task** drawing on §3.3.4 change, §3.2.3 culture and relevant finance/strategy. It does not claim that a question from an actual 7138 paper uses this exact tariff or wording; validate the final specification's 15-mark demand before publication.

## Indicative, Revision-authored marking guidance (NOT an AQA mark scheme)

Credit well-developed, applied reasoning and a supported judgement. Possible approaches:
- **Proceed:** competitor pressure plus 8% fall in bookings may justify prompt response; central tracking improves visibility/capacity; staff participation and a staged pilot could improve reliability.
- **Delay or stage:** £95k cost and modest reserves create liquidity exposure; inadequate training could cause errors and staff resistance; a rushed national rollout may disrupt repairs.
- **Kotter/Schlesinger resistance:** worries over overtime are self-interest; staff needing training reflects low tolerance/capability; managers' schedule challenge may represent a different assessment. Communication alone does not resolve financial loss.
- **Lewin:** identify customer demand/competition as drivers, cost/staff skill/culture as restraints; pilot, training and negotiation lower restraints. A subjective score alone is not evidence of benefit.
- **Decision:** compare predicted commercial upside against cash constraints, safety, feasibility and alternative rollout design; justify a conditional decision, e.g. a measured two-branch pilot with firm go/no-go milestones.

**Experimental performance descriptors** (require independent calibration): strong (12–15): sustained, context-rich analysis and justified balanced judgement; developing (8–11): sound but uneven evaluation; basic (4–7): some applied points with limited explanation; limited (1–3): fragmentary statements; 0: nothing relevant. These are not official bands; a qualified reviewer must confirm compatibility with 7138's levels-based AO weighting before using them to mark student answers.

**Teacher review must check:** actual tariff/AO split; whether figures and case permit a genuinely discriminating judgement; legitimacy of suggested force-field applications; whether trained staff or annual booking decline assumptions are unsupported extrapolations; whether wrong 7132-only frameworks have been treated as mandatory 7138.
