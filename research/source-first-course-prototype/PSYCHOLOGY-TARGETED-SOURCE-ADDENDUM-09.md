DOI: `10.3389/fpsyg.2021.758623`  
URL: https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.758623  
Classification: `OPEN`

Supports Jahoda’s six elements of positive mental health:

- positive attitudes toward the self;
- growth, development and self-actualisation;
- integration / resilience and resistance to stress;
- autonomy;
- realistic perception of reality; and
- environmental mastery, including effective adaptation and functioning.

### Course Truth bridge

Psychological abnormality or poor mental health can be approached using different reference standards rather than one universally sufficient definition:

- **statistical infrequency** asks whether a characteristic or behaviour is unusual relative to a population distribution;
- **deviation from social/cultural norms** asks whether behaviour breaches expectations or conventions in a relevant social context;
- **failure to function adequately** focuses on whether a person can manage important ordinary demands or experiences clinically significant dysfunction/distress; and
- **deviation from ideal mental health** starts from positive criteria for healthy functioning, such as self-acceptance, growth, integration, autonomy, realistic perception and environmental mastery.

Each definition captures something useful but can misclassify people if treated as sufficient on its own. Statistical rarity is not automatically disorder; norms vary across cultures and time; functional impairment depends on context; and an ideal-health standard can set a demanding positive benchmark that many psychologically healthy people will not meet in every respect.

**Why this closes the bridge:** the accepted abnormal-psychology corpus already covered three definition families. The additional `CC BY` source supplies Jahoda’s positive-functioning criteria directly, allowing the fourth definition to be independently explained without copying AQA prose.

## PSY-04-03 — behavioural explanation and treatment of phobias

**Previous live status:** `partial`  
**Continuation status:** `covered_candidate`

### Existing accepted sources

BCcampus *Psychology — H5P Edition* already supplies `CC BY 4.0` foundations for:

- classical conditioning;
- operant conditioning and reinforcement; and
- exposure-based treatment / systematic desensitisation with relaxation and graduated fear-provoking stimuli.

### Additional accepted source

Norberg, Visvalingam, Stevenson & Saluja (2024), *A review of the phenomenology, aetiology and treatment of animal phobia and insights for biophobia*  
DOI: `10.1002/pan3.10514`  
URL: https://doi.org/10.1002/pan3.10514  
Classification: `OPEN`  
Licence: `CC BY`  
Rights evidence: the publisher/repository record identifies the final published article as open access under Creative Commons Attribution.  
Supports:

- Pavlovian/conditioning contributions to phobia development;
- behavioural maintenance through avoidance/escape and reinforcement;
- systematic desensitisation as graduated exposure paired historically with relaxation;
- flooding as prolonged exposure to high-intensity feared stimuli;
- extinction/habituation and contemporary inhibitory-learning accounts of exposure; and
- an important evaluation qualification that exposure is effective overall but mechanisms and outcomes are not uniform or guaranteed.

### Course Truth bridge

The **two-process model** is a useful course structure for separating phobia acquisition from maintenance:

1. **Acquisition:** through classical conditioning, a previously neutral cue can become associated with an aversive event and acquire the capacity to evoke fear.
2. **Maintenance:** avoidance or escape can reduce fear/anxiety in the short term; that relief reinforces avoidance, reducing opportunities for corrective learning and helping the phobia persist.

Behavioural treatments use exposure to change the learned fear relationship:

- **systematic desensitisation** uses a graded hierarchy of feared situations, traditionally combined with relaxation/counterconditioning, so progressively more difficult cues are approached rather than avoided; and
- **flooding** uses prolonged exposure to a high-intensity feared situation without the gradual hierarchy characteristic of systematic desensitisation, continuing long enough for fear to reduce and/or new non-threat learning to occur.

Modern evidence cautions against teaching treatment mechanisms as a single simple process. Habituation, extinction, expectancy change, inhibitory learning, therapist delivery and avoidance/safety behaviours can all matter.

**Why this closes the bridge:** the previous corpus already supplied conditioning fundamentals and systematic desensitisation. The additional `CC BY` review directly supplies flooding and the exposure/avoidance mechanisms needed to complete the exact course treatment.
