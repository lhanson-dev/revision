## PSY-06-02 — neurons, synaptic transmission, excitation and inhibition

**Previous live status:** `partial`  
**Continuation status:** `covered_candidate`

### Existing accepted sources

BCcampus *Psychology — H5P Edition* (`CC BY 4.0`) already supports sensory and motor neurons, action potentials, synapses, neurotransmitters, receptors and reuptake.

Dalhousie *Introduction to Psychology & Neuroscience (2nd Edition)* (`CC BY 4.0`) independently supports the remaining bridge:

- the spinal interneuron function linking incoming sensory signals to motor output in a reflex pathway;
- excitatory postsynaptic effects as depolarising influences and inhibitory effects as hyperpolarising influences;
- glutamate as an important excitatory neurotransmitter and GABA as an important inhibitory neurotransmitter; and
- EPSP/IPSP integration at the receiving neuron.

### Course Truth bridge

The AQA label **relay neuron** is retained as qualification framing for the independently sourced interneuron function: a neuron within the central nervous system that passes information between other neurons, including between sensory and motor pathways. Sensory neurons carry information toward the central nervous system; motor neurons carry commands from the central nervous system to effectors.

At a chemical synapse, neurotransmitter released from the presynaptic neuron binds to receptors on the postsynaptic cell. The effect can be excitatory, increasing the likelihood of firing, or inhibitory, decreasing it. Neural output reflects integration of multiple excitatory and inhibitory inputs rather than a single transmitter acting in isolation.

## PSY-06-05 — event-related potentials

**Previous live status:** `partial`  
**Continuation status:** `covered_candidate`

### Existing accepted source

Dalhousie *Introduction to Psychology & Neuroscience (2nd Edition)* (`CC BY 4.0`) already supports fMRI, EEG and post-mortem examination, including uses and limitations.

### Additional accepted source

Steven J. Luck (2022), *Applied Event-Related Potential Data Analysis*  
DOI: `10.18115/D5QG92`  
URL: https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Psychology/Biological_Psychology/Applied_Event-Related_Potential_Data_Analysis_%28Luck%29  
Classification: `OPEN`  
Licence: `CC BY 4.0`  
Rights evidence: the LibreTexts work and its constituent pages explicitly state `CC BY 4.0`.  
Supports:

- the relationship between continuously recorded EEG and event-related potentials;
- time-locking EEG activity to defined sensory, cognitive or motor events;
- averaging across repeated trials to isolate event-related voltage patterns from unrelated background activity;
- ERP waveforms, component amplitude/latency and scalp distribution; and
- methodological cautions around noise, artefacts, filtering and interpretation.

### Course Truth bridge

An **EEG** records changes in electrical activity at the scalp over time. An **ERP** is derived from EEG by aligning recordings to repeated occurrences of a defined event and averaging across trials, allowing activity reliably associated with that event to emerge from unrelated background activity. ERPs therefore offer fine temporal information about processing but relatively limited spatial localisation compared with methods such as fMRI.

## PSY-06-06 — localisation, lateralisation, split brain, plasticity and recovery

**Previous live status:** `partial`

### Course Truth bridge

**Localisation** means that some functions depend disproportionately on particular neural regions, while **lateralisation** means that some functions are relatively specialised toward one hemisphere. Primary motor, somatosensory, visual and auditory cortices are examples of functionally specialised cortical regions; Broca’s and Wernicke’s areas provide classic language examples, while modern interpretation avoids treating complex behaviour as the product of isolated modules.

Split-brain research provides evidence for hemispheric specialisation because severing the corpus callosum prevents normal transfer of some information between hemispheres. The findings do not support a simplistic “left-brained/right-brained personality” account.

**Neuroplasticity** allows neural organisation to change with experience, learning or damage. After injury, recovery can involve functional reorganisation and new or strengthened connections, but the degree of recovery varies with the location and severity of damage, age, rehabilitation and other individual factors. Plasticity therefore creates recovery potential rather than guaranteeing restoration of lost function.
