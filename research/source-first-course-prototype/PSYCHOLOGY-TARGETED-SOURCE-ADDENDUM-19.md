### Course Truth bridge

Attraction and relationship formation are shaped by repeated contact/proximity, similarity, self-disclosure and physical attractiveness. The **matching hypothesis** proposes that people often form relationships with partners they perceive as roughly similar to themselves in physical attractiveness/social desirability rather than simply selecting the most objectively attractive available person.

AQA's **filter theory** is retained as a historical model that organises partner selection into progressively narrower filters: **social demography** (who is realistically encountered within social/geographical networks), **similarity of attitudes/values**, and later **complementarity** of some needs or interaction styles. The underlying evidence supports strong roles for proximity and similarity; evidence that broad “opposites attract” or complementarity generally replaces similarity is much weaker and dimension-specific. The model is therefore taught as a useful historical framework rather than a universal sequential law.

### Course Truth bridge

**Social exchange theory** models relationship evaluation in terms of perceived rewards/benefits and costs, with expectations/comparison standards affecting satisfaction. **Equity theory** adds fairness: people compare their own input-to-outcome relationship with their partner's; perceived under-benefit or over-benefit can create distress even when a relationship has net rewards.

Rusbult's **investment model** explains commitment using three principal predictors: relationship satisfaction, quality of alternatives and investment size. Greater satisfaction and investment and poorer alternatives generally predict stronger commitment. Commitment is probabilistic and descriptive; investment should never be used to imply that someone ought to remain in an unsafe or harmful relationship.

Duck's AQA-named phases describe relationship dissolution as a process: **intra-psychic** private dissatisfaction/reflection; **dyadic** direct discussion/negotiation between partners; **social** involvement of the wider social network and public consequences; and **grave-dressing** post-breakup sense-making and construction of an account of the relationship's ending. Stage/phase models are heuristic: real breakups can be abrupt, recursive, skip phases or end in repair rather than dissolution.

### Course Truth bridge

Online communication changes the **gates** operating during early relationship formation. Physical appearance, immediate non-verbal behaviour, geographical distance and some visible social characteristics can be reduced, delayed or selectively presented. AQA calls this **absence of gating**. It does not mean that online relationships have no barriers: platforms create new gates through profiles, algorithms, access, reputation, language and digital literacy.

Online settings can support rapid or deep self-disclosure for some users because they offer greater control over timing and self-presentation and can reduce immediate fear of evaluation. The same control makes selective or deceptive self-presentation easier. Online identity can therefore range from authentic selective disclosure to exaggeration or deliberate falsehood; deception is possible offline too and is not an inevitable feature of computer-mediated relationships.

URL: https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00255  
Classification: `OPEN`  
Licence: `CC BY`  
Supports attachment as an individual-difference framework used to explain variation in parasocial involvement, with findings not fully consistent across attachment dimensions.

### Course Truth bridge

A **parasocial relationship** is a psychologically meaningful but structurally one-sided bond with a media figure or character who does not reciprocate the individual relationship. Such bonds are common and can support entertainment, identity exploration, belonging and comfort; parasocial involvement is not inherently pathological.

The AQA-linked celebrity-worship continuum distinguishes **entertainment-social** involvement, **intense-personal** emotional preoccupation and **borderline-pathological** extreme attitudes/behaviour. The **absorption-addiction model** proposes that some people become increasingly absorbed in a celebrity relationship to meet identity or relational needs, with escalating involvement potentially requiring more intense engagement. This is a model of extreme involvement, not a claim that ordinary fandom inevitably progresses toward pathology.

Attachment explanations propose that internal working models and attachment insecurity may affect the appeal or intensity of safe, controllable one-sided bonds. Evidence more consistently links attachment anxiety with stronger parasocial involvement than it supports one simple insecure-attachment rule, and findings for avoidance are mixed. Attachment theory should therefore be taught as a probabilistic explanation, not a diagnosis of fans.

## Interpretation

Relationships was unusually fragmented because several AQA-named models are older specialist taxonomies not reproduced in the broad open psychology textbooks. The rights-safe route is to preserve those theory names as qualification alignment while building the actual explanations from openly licensed relationship, communication and media-psychology evidence. The resulting Course Truth should explicitly distinguish historical models from stronger contemporary evidence, especially for filter theory, phase models, online gating and parasocial attachment.
