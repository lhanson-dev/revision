# Lesson: Why memory is useful — and why it sometimes fails

## Learning goals

By the end you should be able to compare the three stores in the multi-store model, explain each working-memory component and its coding/capacity, distinguish two interference mechanisms from retrieval failure, and evaluate how questioning, other witnesses, anxiety and cognitive interviewing affect eyewitness accuracy.

### 1. The multi-store model (MSM)

Imagine hearing a six-digit code and then having to enter it a few seconds later. The information reaches your senses, needs your attention and may soon disappear. The **multi-store model**, commonly associated with Atkinson and Shiffrin, describes memory as information handled by three distinguishable stores:

- **Sensory register:** raw sensory traces last very briefly. Visual (iconic) information is typically extremely short-lived; auditory (echoic) information may persist a little longer. Capacity is large for the immediate incoming scene but only a small fraction is selected for further processing.
- **Short-term memory (STM):** holds a relatively small amount of information for seconds unless kept active. Acoustic coding can dominate when remembering verbal items, although coding depends on task. You may recall the sound of a word but confuse similarly sounding items.
- **Long-term memory (LTM):** knowledge and experiences may last years; practical capacity is extremely extensive. Semantic coding often matters for verbal knowledge, but long-term memories can also include sensory and procedural information.

Three distinct *features* must be compared for each store: **coding** (how information is represented), **capacity** (how much can be held) and **duration** (how long it persists). These features are not interchangeable. Older classic estimates, such as seven plus or minus two chunks for STM, are task-dependent approximations, not unchanging universal limits.

**Key idea:** attention lets selected sensory input be processed, and rehearsal can support STM retention and transfer into LTM in the original account; however, not everything rehearsed becomes long-term knowledge, and meaningful encoding also matters.

**Evaluation:** The model helpfully distinguishes different memory performances, but it oversimplifies STM as one store and LTM as one uniform system. A person may show weaker verbal short-term processing than visual processing. That pattern calls for a richer model.

**Check:** A pupil hears a telephone number once and retains it for twelve seconds without writing it down. Which store mainly explains the temporary holding, and what might prevent accurate recall? *STM; capacity limits, interference and lack of active maintenance could reduce retention.*

### 2. The working-memory model (WMM)

The WMM describes working memory as **active temporary storage and processing**, rather than one passive short-term container. It includes interacting components:

| Component | Main function | Coding / capacity implications |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Central executive | Controls attention and coordinates resources | Capacity is limited; best understood as attentional control, not a large word store |
| Phonological loop | Maintains and rehearses speech-based material | Mainly acoustic/verbal coding; limited amount can be maintained at once |
| Visuo-spatial sketchpad | Holds/manipulates shapes, images, positions | Primarily visual and spatial coding; limited capacity |
| Episodic buffer | Temporarily integrates information from components and LTM | Multimodal binding into coherent episodes; limited capacity |

**Worked example:** While following spoken driving directions, a learner can hold verbal instructions in the phonological loop and picture a route using the sketchpad. But simultaneously reading complicated instructions aloud may interfere with verbal rehearsal. The executive allocates attention. The episodic buffer can connect details into a single meaningful journey.

**Evidence and evaluation:** When two tasks compete for the same limited processing resources, performance often suffers more than when they rely partly on different systems. This supports functional distinctions within working memory. However, the central executive is hard to specify precisely: saying it “controls attention” explains *what* it does more clearly than *how* it does it. The model is useful without proving that its components correspond to four neat anatomical locations.

**Misconception:** the phonological loop is *not* the whole of STM, and the episodic buffer does *not* permanently store episodes.

### 3. Forgetting: interference and retrieval failure

You learn a French translation on Monday, then learn a similar Spanish translation on Tuesday. On Wednesday the two compete.

- **Proactive interference:** earlier learning disrupts later learning or its retrieval — *old interferes with new*.
- **Retroactive interference:** later learning disrupts recall of earlier learning — *new interferes with old*.

The risk is greater where memories share similar cues or content. But not all forgetting is interference: sometimes a memory is stored but cannot be reached using the cues available at the time.

**Retrieval failure due to missing cues:** According to the *encoding specificity* principle, recall is often better if retrieval cues meaningfully match features that were present when a memory was encoded. Cues might concern a place, mood or distinctive associated information. The effect is conditional: returning to the same room cannot guarantee recall.

**Worked comparison:** Forgetting Monday's French term because Tuesday's Spanish term comes to mind is likely **retroactive interference**. Remembering a friend's name only after being reminded where you met can illustrate **cue-dependent retrieval**.

**Evaluation:** Interference explains competition between similarly encoded material but cannot account for every lost memory. Cue-based accounts explain why recall can improve with an appropriate prompt, yet they should not claim that any cue or context reinstatement always works.

### 4. Eyewitness testimony: memory is reconstructive

Witnesses remember and report an event through attention, encoding, later information and retrieval. A confident witness can still be mistaken.

**Leading questions:** a question containing an assumption or suggestion can change what is reported or remembered. For example, “Which side of the vehicle was damaged?” already assumes damage existed. Avoid inferring from such effects that all questions permanently change memory.

**Post-event discussion:** people who discuss the same event may import another person's information into their own report or feel pressure to agree. This is a possible source of memory conformity, not a rule that discussion always makes testimony worse.

**Anxiety:** strong emotion can narrow attention towards some central details while weakening attention to others. Studies vary by setting and timing; neither “anxiety always improves recall” nor “anxiety always reduces it” is defensible. Distinguish memory for central versus peripheral details and consider the difference between real incidents and laboratory tasks.

**Cognitive interview:** an interviewer can support recall using:
1. **Reinstate context mentally:** think about sights, sounds, sequence and feelings around the original event.
2. **Report everything:** include details initially thought insignificant, without guessing.
3. **Recall in another order:** try a non-chronological order to interrupt routine reconstruction.
4. **Recall from another perspective:** attempt alternative viewpoints cautiously; speculative invention must not be treated as fact.

**Evaluation:** Properly used interviewing methods can elicit more useful information than ordinary questioning, but they take training and time. Any extra detail still requires accuracy checking. The alternative-perspective technique may be particularly vulnerable to speculation if poorly administered.

### Putting the ideas together

Suppose two students witness a bag taken from a changing room. One student is frightened and focuses on the person's jacket. Later, their friend insists the jacket was black although they initially remembered blue. During an interview, a neutral prompt about the layout of the room may help retrieve details.

Here the relevant concepts are **selective attention under anxiety**, **post-event discussion**, **potential suggestibility** and **context cues in retrieval**. A strong explanation identifies *which* mechanism accounts for *which* detail; simply saying “memory is unreliable” is too vague.

## Quick recap

- MSM: sensory register, STM, LTM; compare coding, capacity and duration.
- WMM: executive, phonological loop, visuo-spatial sketchpad and episodic buffer; link components to task demands.
- Forgetting: distinguish old-on-new from new-on-old and both from a failure to retrieve with effective cues.
- Eyewitness: questioning, co-witness discussion, anxiety and the cognitive interview affect accuracy in different, conditional ways.

# 10-question quiz — original, with answers and feedback

*Choose one best answer for each question. 1 mark per item; this is Revision-authored practice, not an official board quiz.*

1. **Which property means the *form* in which memory is stored?** A Capacity; B Duration; C Coding; D Retrieval. **Answer C.** Coding refers to representation, not amount or time.
2. **A witness briefly registers a flash of light before attending to anything else. Which store is most relevant?** A Sensory register; B LTM; C Episodic buffer; D Phonological loop. **Answer A.** Very brief sensory traces are distinct from attended STM content.
3. **Which claim about STM is safest?** A It always holds exactly seven items; B Capacity depends in part on material and task; C It encodes only visually; D It stores every input permanently. **Answer B.** Fixed universal claims exaggerate classic estimates.
4. **Mentally rotating an unfamiliar floor plan principally engages which component?** A Phonological loop; B Episodic memory; C Central executive alone; D Visuo-spatial sketchpad. **Answer D.** Visual-spatial transformation is sketchpad-intensive.
5. **Which is the clearest role of the episodic buffer?** A Repeating a postcode silently; B Holding only sounds; C Integrating visual, verbal and long-term information temporarily; D Storing autobiographical memories permanently. **Answer C.** It binds information and is not permanent storage.
6. **A pupil remembers an old password but struggles to retrieve a recently changed password.** A Retroactive interference; B Proactive interference; C Post-event discussion; D Cue-independent forgetting. **Answer B.** Old information disrupts the newer target.
7. **A newly memorised dance sequence disrupts recall of a previously learned sequence.** A Retroactive interference; B Sensory persistence; C Proactive interference; D Memory conformity. **Answer A.** New-on-old is retroactive.
8. **Which observation most directly supports cue-dependent retrieval?** A Familiar contextual clues improve recall of a previously studied item; B Every repeated word is stored; C Longer storage always improves recall; D Every witness agrees. **Answer A.** A meaningful encoding–retrieval cue match can help recall.
9. **Two witnesses discuss an event and later share the same mistaken detail. Which risk is illustrated?** A Central-executive overload only; B Higher iconic capacity; C Increased STM duration; D Post-event discussion / conformity. **Answer D.** One account can contaminate another.
10. **Which is an appropriate cognitive-interview practice?** A Tell the witness what others said; B Encourage useful contextual recall without pressuring them to guess; C Demand certainty for all details; D Omit time-consuming neutral prompts. **Answer B.** Better recall requires neutral, non-leading interviewing.

## Indicative content and marking dimensions — provisional, not an AQA mark scheme

**Provisional teaching allocation: AO1 6; AO3 10.** A qualified reviewer must validate the exact allocation and levels against the applicable 2027 assessment profile before this can calibrate marking.

**AO1 — knowledge (up to 6):** Accurate, connected explanation of the central executive's attentional control, phonological loop and acoustic-verbal processing, visuo-spatial sketchpad, episodic buffer, and their limited-capacity/processing relationships. Reward specific explanations of coding and capacity, not just naming boxes.

**AO3 — evaluation (up to 10):** Well-reasoned strengths and limitations, for example:
- Concurrent-task interference as evidence that working-memory components are functionally distinguishable; distinguish the prediction from the evidence.
- How evidence of verbal–visual dissociations challenges a unitary STM account.
- The executive's broad/under-specified functions and difficulty of direct measurement.
- The need to avoid implying four physically separate stores or fixed universal capacities.
- Weigh evidence and explain whether the model is more useful than a single-store STM approach.

**Original response-outline example:** Explain the components and how they interact in a meaningful example. Develop two evaluation paragraphs that connect dual-task findings and differentiated coding to the model's central claim. Then discuss the central executive's limited specification and assess whether this weakens the model's explanatory detail rather than invalidating the useful parts. Finish by judging how far the model improves upon a unitary STM store.

**Experimental bands (to be calibrated, not official):** 13–16: accurate, integrated components and sustained evidence-based evaluation; 9–12: mostly accurate with reasoned but uneven evaluation; 5–8: partial detail and predominantly descriptive points; 1–4: fragmentary knowledge and weak relevance; 0: no creditworthy response. Within a band, assess precision, completeness, justification and clarity. No automatic award based on keyword counting.

**Common marking pitfalls:** Giving a full score for listing components without evaluation; confusing episodic buffer with permanent episodic LTM; assuming all processing is purely acoustic; treating divided-task research as conclusive proof of anatomical modules.
