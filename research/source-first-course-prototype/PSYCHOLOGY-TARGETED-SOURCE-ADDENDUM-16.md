## PSY-14-03 — life changes, daily hassles and workplace stress

**Previous live status:** `partial`  
**Continuation status:** `covered_candidate`

### Accepted source

BCcampus, *Psychology — H5P Edition*, “Stressors”  
URL: https://opentextbc.ca/h5ppsychology/chapter/stressors/  
Classification: `OPEN`  
Licence: `CC BY 4.0`

Supports:

- significant life changes as potential stressors;
- the Social Readjustment Rating Scale as a way of operationalising life change;
- daily hassles as repeated minor stressors;
- job strain arising especially where heavy demands/workload combine with limited discretion or control; and
- the need to distinguish stressor exposure from individual appraisal.

### Course Truth bridge

Stress can arise from major life changes, repeated everyday hassles and chronic workplace demands. Life-change approaches emphasise the cumulative adjustment required by events; daily-hassles approaches focus on frequent minor irritants; workplace models show that high demands combined with low control can create job strain. These are overlapping routes to stress rather than mutually exclusive categories.

## PSY-14-04 — SRRS, Hassles and Uplifts, and physiological measurement

**Previous live status:** `partial`  
**Continuation status:** `covered_candidate`

### Accepted sources

BCcampus, *Psychology — H5P Edition*, “Stressors”  
URL: https://opentextbc.ca/h5ppsychology/chapter/stressors/  
Classification: `OPEN`  
Licence: `CC BY 4.0`  
Supports the SRRS, life-change units, daily hassles and correlational cautions.

Mroczek & Almeida (open-access PMC record), *Do Hassles and Uplifts Change with Age?*  
URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4271845/  
Classification: `OPEN`  
Licence: open-access article; reusable factual extraction only unless article-level licence conditions are separately confirmed for expression.  
Supports the 53-item Hassles and Uplifts Scale structure, separate hassle/uplift ratings, exposure/frequency and intensity scoring.

Westerink et al. (2020), *Deriving a Cortisol-Related Stress Indicator From Wearable Skin Conductance Measurements*  
URL: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/computer-science/articles/10.3389/fcomp.2020.00039/full  
Classification: `OPEN`  
Licence: `CC BY 4.0`  
Supports skin conductance as a physiological stress/arousal measure and the relationship between rapid electrodermal change and slower endocrine stress markers.

Frontiers psychology psychophysiology literature also supports skin conductance response as the phasic component of electrodermal activity driven by sympathetic sweat-gland activity.

### Course Truth bridge

The SRRS quantifies accumulated life change using weighted life-change units. The Hassles and Uplifts Scale instead samples everyday negative and positive experiences and can score how many occur and how strongly they are experienced. Physiological approaches measure bodily correlates such as skin conductance, heart rate/heart-rate variability or cortisol.

No measure is a direct meter of “stress itself”. Self-report scales depend on appraisal and recall; physiological measures are more objective but are not stress-specific because arousal can occur for many reasons. Skin conductance response reflects short-lived sympathetic electrodermal changes and should therefore be interpreted as an arousal/reactivity indicator rather than a unique diagnostic marker of psychological stress.

## PSY-14-05 — Type A/B/C and hardiness

**Previous live status:** `partial`  
**Continuation status:** `covered_candidate`

### Accepted sources

BCcampus, *Psychology — H5P Edition*, “Stress and Illness”  
URL: https://opentextbc.ca/h5ppsychology/chapter/stress-and-illness/  
Classification: `OPEN`  
Licence: `CC BY 4.0`  
Supports Type A and Type B behaviour patterns and the later evidence that hostility/anger is more informative than a simple Type A category for cardiovascular risk.

Rymarczyk et al. (2020), *Type C Personality: Conceptual Refinement and Preliminary Operationalization*  
URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7525034/  
Classification: `OPEN`  
Licence: `CC BY`  
Supports Type C as a proposed construct involving patterns such as emotional suppression and compliance, while also documenting conceptual/measurement uncertainty.

Vagni et al. (2022), *Emergency Stress, Hardiness, Coping Strategies and Burnout...*  
URL: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.918788/full  
Classification: `OPEN`  
Licence: `CC BY`  
Supports Kobasa-style hardiness as commitment, control and challenge, plus evidence that hardiness can buffer adverse stress outcomes and is associated with more adaptive coping.

### Course Truth bridge

Type A describes a behaviour pattern involving competitiveness, time urgency, impatience and hostility; Type B is the comparatively more relaxed pattern. The original claim that a broad Type A category strongly predicts coronary disease did not replicate consistently, and later work points more specifically to anger/hostility as a relevant risk factor.

Type C is a historically proposed personality pattern often described in terms of emotional suppression, conflict avoidance/compliance and related coping tendencies. It should not be presented as an established cause of cancer or disease.

Hardiness describes a resilience-related pattern organised around **commitment**, **control** and **challenge**. It is best taught as a probabilistic protective resource associated with adaptive coping, not as immunity from stress.

URL: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/behavioral-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnbeh.2016.00228/full  
Classification: `OPEN`  
Licence: `CC BY`  
Supports stress-buffering social support and explicitly distinguishes esteem, informational/companionship and instrumental support.

BCcampus accepted therapy material remains sufficient for the already-supported drug-management foundation; medication should be treated as symptom/physiological management with benefit-risk considerations rather than a universal coping strategy.

### Course Truth bridge

Stress Inoculation Training is a cognitive-behavioural programme that builds understanding of stress, rehearses coping skills and then applies those skills under increasingly realistic stress conditions. Biofeedback measures a physiological signal and returns that information to the user so self-regulation skills can be learned; effectiveness depends on method, context and training.

Gender differences in coping are group-level findings, not rules for individuals. Social support can buffer stress through several functions: **instrumental** help (practical/tangible assistance), **emotional** support (care and understanding) and **esteem** support (affirmation of worth and competence). These functions can overlap in real relationships.
