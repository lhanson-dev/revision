# Teen Audience UX Research — 15 to 18 Year Olds

**Status:** Research evidence — non-authoritative  
**Source:** Founder-provided research report, 17 August 2026  
**Audience:** UK teenagers aged approximately 15–18  
**Purpose:** Preserve the research findings used to inform Revision product, experience, trust and accessibility decisions.

> This document is research evidence. It does not override approved Revision authority. Findings become normative only when deliberately promoted into the appropriate governance document.

## Executive finding

The research argues that effective digital experiences for 15–18-year-olds should be clear, concise, engaging, mobile-friendly, fast, trustworthy, accessible and respectful. Teenagers have low patience for unnecessary complexity and dense reading. Visuals and interaction can help engagement, but experiences should not become cluttered, childish, gimmicky or patronising.

## Content and cognition

Key findings:

- teenagers tend to scan rather than read long pages in depth;
- use short chunks, clear headings, brief paragraphs and action-oriented labels;
- use plain, age-appropriate language and avoid unexplained jargon;
- break complex tasks into smaller steps;
- use progressive disclosure so deeper detail is available without overwhelming the primary task;
- use visuals, video and interactive elements to support rather than replace understanding;
- retain optional deeper information where concision would otherwise remove useful nuance.

## Visual design and tone

Key findings:

- avoid both sterile institutional design and childish visual treatment;
- keep layouts clean and uncluttered with strong hierarchy and readable controls;
- use a friendly, one-to-one, respectful tone;
- do not lecture or talk down to teenagers;
- avoid trying too hard to sound young through slang, memes or forced youth language;
- colour and imagery can create energy, but should not undermine clarity, accessibility or credibility.

## Navigation and onboarding

Key findings:

- make the main action immediately obvious;
- keep navigation relatively flat and recognisable;
- avoid complicated menus, excessive pop-ups and deep nesting;
- minimise onboarding and collect information progressively;
- explain why information is requested;
- where practical, allow value to be experienced before demanding unnecessary account setup;
- provide contextual help for unfamiliar terms;
- support both guided pathways and flexible exploration.

## Mobile and performance

Key findings:

- design mobile-first rather than treating mobile as a reduced desktop experience;
- use large, touch-friendly controls and avoid hover-dependent interactions;
- prioritise fast loading, including on older devices and weaker connections;
- avoid unnecessary autoplay, heavy animation or media that harms responsiveness;
- balance rich content formats with performance.

## Trust, privacy and safeguarding

Key findings:

- privacy should be high by default for younger users;
- collect only information that is genuinely needed;
- explain data use in clear, teenager-friendly language;
- do not force public profiles or social sharing;
- provide transparency and user control over sharing;
- provide accessible routes for help and reporting problems;
- visibly communicate credibility, source quality and freshness where relevant;
- product decisions affecting children should consider the UK Age Appropriate Design Code and wider safeguarding obligations.

Legal and compliance claims in the source report should be independently verified before being treated as formal Revision compliance authority.

## Accessibility and neurodiversity

Key findings:

- target WCAG AA accessibility as a baseline experience standard;
- provide readable typography, logical headings, keyboard access and adequate contrast;
- never rely on colour alone to communicate meaning;
- caption video/audio and provide transcripts where appropriate;
- avoid flashing and unnecessarily distracting interactions;
- use clear literal language and consistent layouts;
- consider needs associated with dyslexia, ADHD, autism and other cognitive differences;
- test with a diverse group of teenagers rather than assuming one learner profile.

## Engagement

Key findings:

- interaction is valuable when it has personal relevance and educational purpose;
- quizzes, progress indicators and lightweight game-like feedback can create momentum;
- avoid addictive mechanics, dark patterns or manipulation;
- optional sharing can be useful, but should never be required;
- genuine progress, feedback and relevance are stronger foundations for engagement than gimmicks.

## Parents and guardians

Key findings:

- parents can be given clear information about safety, privacy and how the service works;
- parent involvement should remain supportive rather than patronising or restrictive;
- linked parent experiences require explicit privacy and data-sharing design rather than assuming unrestricted access to a teenager's detailed activity.

## Reference services reviewed in the source

The report considers patterns from services including GOV.UK Find an Apprenticeship, BBC Bitesize, Childline, YoungMinds, The Mix and Rock the Vote. Common strengths include strong calls to action, mobile-friendly layouts, clear language, interactive content and visible trust cues. Common weaknesses include excessive content density, institutional blandness, overly young visual treatment and complexity.

## Revision implications identified in the August 2026 foundation review

The research reinforces existing Revision decisions to:

1. make the next useful action prominent;
2. keep the learner experience calm and uncluttered;
3. offer multiple learning formats;
4. make engagement part of educational effectiveness;
5. keep the tone contemporary and supportive without pretending to be a teenager;
6. make mobile a first-class experience;
7. use progressive disclosure rather than dense pages;
8. give feedback after assessment rather than only scores;
9. keep parent and teacher views separate from the student's everyday experience; and
10. avoid forced social or gamification mechanics.

It also provides evidence for stronger authority on accessibility, neurodiversity, privacy, safeguarding and performance.
