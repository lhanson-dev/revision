## TC-09A — HH-Q4 — 9 marks

**Question class:** contextual analysis  
**Question:** `hh-q4`  
**Challenge category:** correct knowledge, weak application/development

### Learner answer

Holding lots of inventory can help a business because it means it is less likely to run out of materials. This means production can continue and customers can get their orders. It can also be a disadvantage because inventory costs money and takes up space. If too much stock is held then some of it could be wasted or damaged. Overall, keeping inventory is useful if the business needs to make sure production does not stop, but it should not hold too much because this could increase costs.

## TC-16B — HH-Q7 — 16 marks

**Question class:** comparative contextual evaluation  
**Question:** `hh-q7`  
**Challenge category:** balanced boundary case

### Learner answer

Using the bank loan would allow Maya and Ben to keep control of HH. This matters because Maya does not want to give an outside investor 20% of the company or a board seat. If the automation succeeds and reduces waste and variable costs, HH could use the extra cash generated to meet the loan repayments without permanently giving away ownership. However, HH already expects a temporary cash deficit if it buys the equipment. Interest and repayments could make that position worse, especially if the premium range does not grow as quickly as expected.

The investor would avoid fixed loan repayments and could therefore protect short-term cash flow. The investor also has e-commerce experience, which could be valuable because HH sells online and wants to grow. On the other hand, Maya and Ben would lose some control and future profits would be shared with another owner.

I would choose the investor if HH's cash-flow forecast shows that loan repayments leave very little safety margin. If the cash deficit is small and temporary, the loan is probably better because the founders retain ownership. The decision therefore depends mainly on how severe the cash-flow pressure is and how valuable the investor's expertise would actually be.

## TC-16C — HH-Q6 — 16 marks

**Question class:** contextual evaluation  
**Question:** `hh-q6`  
**Challenge category:** unusual but valid conditional argument

### Learner answer

HH should be democratic about **how** automation is introduced but not necessarily about **whether** the investment happens. Employees have information management may not have about bottlenecks, waste and the practical problems of cutting and assembly, so consultation could improve the way the new equipment is installed and used. It may also reduce resistance because labour turnover has already risen from 8% to 15% and workers fear for their jobs. If experienced staff leave during implementation, HH could lose skills at exactly the point when it needs people to learn a new production system.

However, full employee participation in the investment decision could slow HH down. Forecast output is already 22,800 units against capacity of 24,000 and complaints about late deliveries have doubled. Management therefore has a strategic reason to resolve the capacity and efficiency problem quickly. Employees may also focus on job security while directors have to consider finance, customer service and long-term competitiveness.

HH should therefore use a consultative democratic approach: management should make the final investment decision, but employees should have genuine influence over implementation, retraining and job redesign. This gives HH the operational benefits of staff knowledge without making a £500,000 strategic decision dependent on a workforce vote.

## TC-20B — HH-Q8 — 20 marks

**Question class:** synoptic evaluation  
**Question:** `hh-q8`  
**Challenge category:** middle/boundary with relevant and irrelevant material

### Learner answer

Operational performance is important to HH because its forecast capacity utilisation is 95%, which means it is close to its maximum output. The late-delivery complaints suggest this pressure may already be affecting customers. If HH invests in automation, capacity could rise and waste could fall, reducing unit costs and making it easier to fulfil orders. This could improve customer satisfaction and profits.

Marketing performance is also important because HH needs customers to want its products. The premium range could attract professional designers who value sustainable materials and customisation. If HH uses relationship marketing, repeat customers may increase and this could improve revenue. Marketing is especially important because competitors are entering the premium segment.

Finance is important as well because all businesses need money, and human resources are also important because workers need to be motivated. Businesses also need good websites, attractive logos, suitable factories and good leadership. If a company has all of these things it is more likely to be successful.

For HH, operations are probably more important at the moment because it already has demand but is experiencing delivery problems. However, in the long term marketing could become more important if the premium market becomes very competitive. Therefore the most important area changes depending on the main constraint facing the business.

## TC-20C — HH-Q8 — 20 marks

**Question class:** synoptic evaluation  
**Question:** `hh-q8`  
**Challenge category:** strong / potentially unusual-valid synthesis

### Learner answer

For HH, operational performance is currently the tighter constraint on long-term success, but saying it is generally more important than marketing would be too simple. HH's sales index has risen faster than the wider market and forecast capacity utilisation is 95%, while complaints about late delivery have doubled. This suggests demand already exists and operations may be preventing HH from converting that demand into satisfied customers and repeat sales. Automation could raise capacity, reduce waste and lower variable cost per unit, so improving operations could protect both service and margin.

However, operational improvements create value only if HH continues to generate profitable demand. The proposed premium range targets designers who care about customisation and sustainable materials. Good marketing could therefore allow HH to charge a higher price and avoid competing mainly on cost. Relationship marketing could also be important because repeat professional customers may be valuable. If competitors enter with better-designed premium offers, extra production capacity alone would not protect HH.

The two functions also interact. Successful premium marketing could worsen HH's existing capacity problem if orders increase faster than production capability. Equally, spending £500,000 on automation before proving premium demand could create financial pressure because HH already forecasts a temporary cash deficit. HR matters too: rising labour turnover and job-security fears could undermine the benefits of new technology if skilled employees leave or resist implementation.

I therefore agree only for HH's present situation. Its immediate operational problems threaten the customer experience despite strong demand, so fixing capacity, delivery reliability and waste should come first. Over the longer term, the priority should shift according to the constraint: once operational reliability is restored, marketing performance may become equally or more important for sustaining growth and defending the premium position. Long-term success depends less on choosing one function permanently and more on preventing one weak function from limiting the others.
