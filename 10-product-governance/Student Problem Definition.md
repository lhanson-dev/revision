# Student Problem Definition

**Status:** Draft authority candidate — v0.1  
**Purpose:** Define the primary student problem Revision should own and the consequences the product is expected to address.

## Core problem

Students are overwhelmed by the breadth of revision and often lack a reliable way to know what to focus on next.

The challenge is not simply access to revision material. Students can have more material than they know what to do with. The harder problem is deciding where to start, what matters most, what has already been covered and what still needs attention.

Revision should own this problem of prioritisation and coverage.

## What commonly goes wrong

When students do not have clear guidance, they may:

- delay starting because the task feels too large;
- revise topics that feel comfortable or familiar rather than the areas that most need attention;
- miss parts of the relevant specification;
- spend too much time passively reading rather than checking whether they can recall or apply what they have learned;
- believe they know more than the available evidence supports;
- leave too much until late and end up cramming; and
- lose motivation because revision feels repetitive, unclear or endless.

These effects reinforce one another. Poor prioritisation can create incomplete coverage, weak confidence and unnecessary stress.

## Engagement is part of the problem

Traditional revision can feel boring, school-like and overly dependent on reading.

Revision should not treat engagement as a separate entertainment problem. Engagement matters because students are less likely to continue with a system that feels tedious, unclear or difficult to start.

The product should therefore make useful revision easier to begin and easier to continue without relying on superficial engagement mechanics.

## Different ways to revise

Students should be offered multiple useful revision formats rather than being forced into one method.

Depending on the subject, topic and available content, this may include:

- concise written explanations;
- videos;
- audio or podcast-style explanations;
- quizzes;
- recall activities;
- tests;
- practice questions; and
- exam-style practice.

The initial product does not need to claim that it can scientifically identify a single ideal learning style for each student. The more defensible goal is to provide meaningful options and use evidence about progress and engagement to improve recommendations over time.

## What Revision should not try to solve initially

Revision is not initially intended to become:

- a system for teaching every subject from first principles;
- a general homework platform;
- a coursework-management product;
- a solution to broad school behaviour or motivation problems;
- a university-admissions platform; or
- a teacher lesson-planning system.

These areas may be reconsidered in future only if they support the core mission rather than dilute it.

## Definition of a solved problem

For the primary student, the problem is being solved when they can open Revision without having to work out for themselves what they need to do next in order to achieve strong and comprehensive coverage of the relevant subject.

Revision should turn an overwhelming specification into a clear sequence of useful actions, while showing enough evidence of coverage and progress for the student to understand why those actions matter.

## Product test

A proposed capability should have a clear answer to at least one of these questions:

1. Does it improve the student's understanding of what to revise next?
2. Does it improve coverage of the relevant specification?
3. Does it provide better evidence about what the student does or does not know?
4. Does it make effective revision easier to start or continue?
5. Does it reduce uncertainty about whether the student is preparing effectively?

If it does none of these, it is unlikely to address the core problem Revision exists to solve.
