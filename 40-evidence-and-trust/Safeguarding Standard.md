# Safeguarding Standard

**Status:** Draft authority candidate — v0.1  
**Purpose:** Define Revision's safeguarding boundary and required product behaviour when a student interaction suggests serious harm, abuse, danger or acute distress.

## Core principle

Revision is an educational revision product and AI tutor. It should be supportive, encouraging and respectful, but it is not a therapist, counsellor, medical professional or emergency service.

Where a student appears to face a credible serious safeguarding risk, the product must prioritise the student's safety over continuing the ordinary revision interaction.

Safeguarding behaviour should remain child-centred, proportionate and transparent. Revision should seek to help the student reach appropriate real-world support without pretending that the product itself can resolve a serious safeguarding situation.

## 1. Role boundary

Revision may:

- acknowledge that a student is upset, stressed or struggling;
- provide ordinary study coaching and encouragement;
- help reduce revision-related overwhelm;
- suggest practical revision breaks or adjustments where appropriate; and
- encourage the student to speak with a trusted person when additional support would be useful.

Revision must not:

- present itself as a therapist, counsellor or medical professional;
- diagnose mental-health conditions;
- provide clinical treatment advice;
- encourage dependence on the AI tutor for emotional or safeguarding support; or
- imply that speaking with Revision is an adequate substitute for appropriate human help in a serious situation.

## 2. Serious safeguarding concerns take priority

If a student interaction reasonably indicates a credible risk of immediate danger, serious harm, abuse, exploitation, or serious harm to themselves or another person, Revision should temporarily move out of its normal revision-coaching mode.

The immediate purpose of the response becomes helping the student take an appropriate next safety step.

The product should not continue with quizzes, revision recommendations or ordinary motivational coaching while an unresolved serious-risk signal is the dominant context.

## 3. Direct towards real-world help

Revision should encourage a student facing a serious safeguarding concern to seek appropriate real-world support.

Depending on the situation, this may include encouraging contact with:

- a trusted adult;
- a parent or guardian where appropriate and safe;
- a teacher, safeguarding lead or other appropriate professional;
- an appropriate specialist support service; or
- emergency services where there appears to be immediate danger.

The exact support routes, wording and escalation guidance must be validated against current UK safeguarding guidance before production use.

Revision should make the next step clear and achievable rather than overwhelming the student with a long list of organisations or instructions.

## 4. Privacy has safeguarding limits

Revision should not promise that every interaction can always remain completely secret if a future deliberately designed safeguarding escalation process requires otherwise.

However, no broader disclosure right should be inferred from this principle.

Before Revision shares safeguarding-sensitive student information with any third party, the product must have a separately approved policy defining:

- what situations can justify disclosure;
- what information may be shared;
- who may receive it;
- the legal and safeguarding basis for doing so;
- how the student is informed where appropriate; and
- how erroneous or ambiguous AI classifications are handled.

Until such a model is deliberately approved and implemented, the existence of a safeguarding concern does not by itself authorise automated third-party disclosure.

## 5. Do not overreact to ordinary student language

Safeguarding responses must be proportionate.

Ordinary exam stress, frustration or teenage expressions should not automatically be treated as evidence of serious harm.

Examples such as:

- "I'm screwed for this exam";
- "I hate revision";
- being upset after a poor result;
- saying schoolwork is stressful; or
- expressing ordinary worry about grades

should normally remain within Revision's supportive coaching behaviour unless the wider context provides stronger evidence of actual risk.

The system should consider context rather than relying on isolated keywords.

A safeguarding mechanism that repeatedly treats normal frustration as crisis language would undermine trust and could discourage honest student interaction.

## 6. No automated third-party reporting in the first product version

Revision's initial safeguarding capability must not automatically contact parents, schools, police, emergency services or other third parties solely because an AI system has classified a conversation as risky.

For the first product version, Revision should:

1. recognise credible serious-risk signals;
2. stop or deprioritise ordinary revision activity where necessary;
3. respond in clear and supportive language;
4. direct the student towards appropriate real-world help; and
5. preserve enough appropriate operational evidence to evaluate whether the safeguarding system is working safely, subject to privacy and retention rules.

Any future human-review or external-escalation mechanism must be deliberately designed, legally reviewed, safeguarding-reviewed, tested for false positives and false negatives, and approved through governance before release.

## 7. Serious-risk responses must be restrained and usable

A safeguarding response should not become a long generic disclaimer.

It should prioritise:

1. immediate safety where relevant;
2. a clear next action;
3. appropriate human or professional support;
4. simple, calm language; and
5. enough explanation for the student to understand why Revision has changed behaviour.

The product should avoid frightening, shaming, interrogating or lecturing the student.

## 8. AI classification is not a safeguarding judgement by itself

Automated detection may help Revision recognise possible safeguarding concerns, but a model score or keyword match is not equivalent to a professional safeguarding assessment.

Safeguarding logic must account for ambiguity, context and the cost of both missed risks and false alarms.

Where a future workflow involves human review, that review should use a deliberately defined safeguarding process rather than treating the AI output as conclusive.

## 9. Safeguarding rules require current external verification

This standard establishes Revision's product stance. It does not itself claim compliance with statutory safeguarding duties.

Before production implementation, the detailed response patterns, thresholds, escalation routes, support information, record-keeping and disclosure rules must be checked against current applicable UK law and safeguarding guidance, and professionally reviewed where appropriate.

Revision should distinguish between guidance applying specifically to schools or colleges and obligations applying to Revision as an independent consumer education service.

## 10. Review and testing

Safeguarding functionality is safety-critical product behaviour.

Before relying on it in production, Revision should test at minimum:

- clear serious-risk examples;
- ambiguous language;
- ordinary exam stress and frustration;
- repeated or escalating signals across a conversation;
- false-positive behaviour;
- false-negative behaviour;
- age-appropriate wording;
- privacy behaviour; and
- failure modes where an AI service is unavailable or uncertain.

Testing should include realistic teenager language rather than only formal safeguarding phrases.

## Decision test

When a safeguarding-related product decision is unclear, prefer the option that:

1. puts the student's welfare ahead of continuing normal product activity;
2. directs serious concerns towards suitable real-world human help;
3. does not pretend Revision is a professional support service;
4. avoids unnecessary disclosure of private student information;
5. avoids overreacting to normal teenage stress or language;
6. does not make automated external reports without an approved escalation framework; and
7. can be defended against current safeguarding guidance and professional review.
